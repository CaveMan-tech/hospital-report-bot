## Executive summary (10 lines)

1. **Solid:** National Health Act 2014, s.20 wording and penalty confirmed from the scanned Official Gazette (No. 145, Vol. 101, 27 Oct 2014, p. A153). The draft's penalty note (N100,000, up to six months, or both) is correct. The gazette says "for any reason" (not "for any reason whatsoever", which many secondary sources add).
2. **Solid:** the law says emergency treatment cannot be REFUSED. It does not say it is FREE. The FCCPC Patients' Bill of Rights says explicitly that emergency prioritisation does "not mean a waiver of obligation to pay". The bot is right never to say "free".
3. **Solid:** Gunshot Act 2017 ss.1, 2, 3, 11 confirmed from the PLAC-hosted certified copy: treat "with or without police clearance" and "with or without initial monetary deposit". It covers gunshot wounds only, not road accidents or stabbings.
4. **Solid:** Patients' Bill of Rights (PBoR) was launched 31 July 2018 by the Consumer Protection Council (now FCCPC) with the Federal Ministry of Health. It is NOT a statute. It describes itself as "an aggregation of patients' rights that exist in other instruments". The official FCCPC illustrated guide is organised in lettered sections A to K, not as a numbered list of 12; the "12 rights" list is the short version.
5. **Corrections to draft rights lines:** (b) merges two different rights and "urgent attention when you need it" overstates the text, which is limited to "the event of an emergency". (c) "and to be heard" is not in the PBoR; the better source is National Health Act s.30(1), a statute, which gives a right to lay a complaint "and have the complaint investigated".
6. **Shaky:** detention over unpaid bills. There is no Nigerian statute that names hospital detention, no appellate decision I could find, and no NHRC statement. The best authority found is one State High Court decision (Anambra, Suit O/255/2017, false imprisonment, 10-hour detention at a mission hospital) seen only in a secondary source, plus Constitution ss.34, 35, 41 (text verified). The draft's cautious wording is appropriate. I found NO Nigerian authority on detention of corpses.
7. **Correction:** the Lagos "Office of the Public Defender (OPD)" now calls itself the "Bureau of the Public Defender (BPD)" on its own sites, though "OPD" is still widely used. Toll-free 0800-006-7352 / 0800-002-7352 (own website). SERVICOM is a FEDERAL mechanism: it applies to LUTH, FMC Ebute-Metta, Igbobi, not to Lagos State general hospitals.
8. **Correction:** the MDCN online complaint form requires an AFFIDAVIT upload (seen on the live form). The NMCN "Complaints Portal" says it is "strictly for technical issues", so it is NOT a misconduct channel; the NMCN "report a nurse" page returns 404. Do not send reporters to it.
9. **UCH Ibadan claims: not verified.** I could not find any paper containing "54 complaints", "about a quarter aware" or "83.7% never asserted rights". The only UCH paper found (Abolarin and Oyetunde 2013) says nearly the opposite (94.2% good knowledge of rights; 75% would seek redress). A 2020 Ondo study gives 46.8% aware of the PBoR and 23.8% aware of its contents. Do not use the three figures.
10. **Could not verify:** any prosecution under NHA s.20; any Lagos State law on emergency care without deposit or police report; the digits behind the "0800 000 LASHMA" vanity number; MANI crisis numbers on MANI's own site (blocked by bot check); whether 767/112 are free on every network.

## A. Emergency treatment law

### A1. National Health Act 2014, section 20

Citation: National Health Act, 2014 (Act No. 8 of 2014), Part III "Rights and Obligations of Users and Healthcare Personnel", s.20, marginal note "Emergency Treatment". Federal Republic of Nigeria Official Gazette No. 145, Vol. 101, Lagos, 27 October 2014, Government Notice No. 208, pp. A139-172; s.20 is at p. A153.

Exact text (read from the gazette scan):

> "20.—(1) A health care provider, health worker or health establishment shall not refuse a person emergency medical treatment for any reason."
>
> "(2) A person who contravenes this section commits an offence and is liable on conviction to a fine of N100,000.00 or to imprisonment for a period not exceeding six months or to both."

- Source (scan of the gazetted Act, 40 pages, s.20 on PDF page 17): https://scorecard.prb.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/06/Nigeria-National-Health-Act-2014.pdf . **HIGH**
- Section-by-section HTML copy for easier reading (secondary): https://www.lawglobalhub.com/nigerian-national-health-act-2014/ and http://lawglobalhub.com/section-20-nigerian-national-health-act-2014/ . MEDIUM
- Note: several secondary sources (SSRN paper, The Guardian Nigeria) quote it as "for any reason whatsoever". The gazette scan does not contain "whatsoever". The draft bot text ("for any reason") matches the gazette. **HIGH**
- Draft internal penalty note ("a fine of N100,000, up to six months' imprisonment, or both"): **supported. HIGH.**
- FREE or only CANNOT BE REFUSED? Section 20 says nothing about cost. It only prohibits refusal. **HIGH.** The PBoR confirms the intent: patient responsibility under "Access to Emergency Care" is to "Recognize that emergency treatment and prioritization do not mean a waiver of obligation to pay for services", and under "Fee Related Information" the provider "has a right to fees and therefore should use legal means to recover fees owed" (FCCPC illustrated guide, pp. 4 and 8). **HIGH**
- "Emergency medical treatment" does not appear to be defined in the Act. Commentators (e.g. Sandra Bello, SSRN 2025, https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=5351066) treat this as a weakness. I did not read the whole interpretation section of the scan. MEDIUM. This matters: a hospital can say "this was not an emergency".
- Related sections read in the same scan: s.23 (user to be given information on health status, options, "benefits, risks, costs and consequences", in a language the user understands), p. A154, **HIGH**; s.21(3) (a provider "may refuse to treat a user who is physically or verbally abusive or who sexually harasses him or her", without prejudice to s.19(1), except psychiatric patients), p. A154, **HIGH**. The bot's advice to "ask calmly" is therefore legally sensible as well as practical.
- s.30 "Laying of Complaints", p. A156: "30.—(1) A person may lay a complaint about the manner in which he or she was treated at a health establishment and have the complaint investigated." s.30(2): the Minister, Commissioner or other authority "shall establish a procedure for the laying of complaints"; s.30(3)(a): the procedure must "be displayed by all health establishments in a manner that is visible for any person entering the establishment". **HIGH**

### A2. Compulsory Treatment and Care for Victims of Gunshots Act, 2017

Source: PLAC-hosted copy certified by the Clerk to the National Assembly (14 Nov 2017), assented by President Buhari (December 2017): https://placng.org/i/wp-content/uploads/2019/12/Compulsory-Treatment-and-Care-for-Victims-of-Gunshots-Act-2017.pdf . **HIGH** (read directly; it is a scan of the certified bill, not the gazette print).

- s.1: "every hospital in Nigeria whether public or private shall accept or receive, for immediate and adequate treatment with or without police clearance, any person with a gunshot wound."
- s.2(2)(a): a person with a gunshot wound "shall be received for immediate and adequate treatment by any hospital in Nigeria with or without initial monetary deposit".
- s.2(2)(b): such a person "shall not be subjected to inhuman and degrading treatment or torture by any person or authority including the police".
- s.3(1): the hospital must report to the nearest police station "within two hours of commencement of treatment". So the duty to report sits with the HOSPITAL, after treatment starts.
- s.5: hospital that fails to report: fine N100,000; doctors directly concerned: six months or N100,000 or both.
- s.11: any person or authority, including police or hospital, "who stands by and fails to perform his duty under this Act which results in the unnecessary death" of a gunshot victim: fine N500,000 or five years or both.
- s.14: High Court shall order restitution to the victim.
- s.15: "victim" covers "gunshot, powder burn and other injuries arising out of or caused by the discharge of a firearm". It does NOT cover road accidents, stabbings or assaults. **HIGH.** For those, only NHA s.20 applies. Do not tell a road-accident reporter that the "Gunshot Act" protects them.
- Citation per s.16: "Compulsory Treatment and Care of Victims of Gunshots Act, 2017" (the long title uses "for"). Both forms circulate.
- Compliance is poor: HumAngle "Despite Laws, Nigerian Hospitals Still Reject Gunshot Victims" (https://humanglemedia.com/despite-laws-nigerian-hospitals-still-reject-gunshot-victims/), FIJ (https://fij.ng/article/why-do-hospitals-continue-to-request-police-reports-for-gunshot-wounds/), peer-reviewed commentary in Medicine, Conflict and Survival 2024 (https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/13623699.2024.2425336). MEDIUM (titles and abstracts only; HumAngle page blocked by a bot check).

## B. Detention of patients and bodies over unpaid bills

### B1. What the law clearly says (text verified)

Constitution of the Federal Republic of Nigeria 1999, copy hosted by the NHRC: https://nigeriarights.gov.ng/files/constitution.pdf . **HIGH** for the quoted words.

- s.34(1): "Every individual is entitled to respect for the dignity of his person, and accordingly (a) no person shall be subject to torture or to inhuman or degrading treatment".
- s.35(1): "Every person shall be entitled to his personal liberty and no person shall be deprived of such liberty save in the following cases and in accordance with a procedure permitted by law", followed by a closed list (a) to (f): court sentence; failure to comply with a court order "or in order to secure the fulfilment of any obligation imposed upon him by law"; bringing before a court on reasonable suspicion of crime; minors' welfare; infectious disease, unsound mind, addiction, vagrancy; immigration. Owing a private debt is not on the list.
- s.41(1): "Every citizen of Nigeria is entitled to move freely throughout Nigeria".
- s.46(1): a person alleging breach may apply to a High Court in that State for redress (Fundamental Rights (Enforcement Procedure) Rules 2009).
- African Charter on Human and Peoples' Rights arts. 5 (dignity) and 6 (liberty), domesticated by Cap A9 LFN 2004. MEDIUM (not re-read today).
- PBoR (FCCPC guide p. 4, Provider Responsibilities under "Fee Related Information"): care providers "have a right to fees and therefore should use legal means to recover fees owed." And p. 10: "Provider may detain patient in the interest of public health." That is the only ground for detention the PBoR mentions. **HIGH.** This is the strongest plain-language line for an "ask": the government's own document says fees are to be recovered by LEGAL MEANS.

### B3. Statutes, directives, NHRC, bills

- No Nigerian statute expressly prohibits hospital detention. **MEDIUM** (absence after a reasonable search).
- I found no NHRC statement specifically on hospital detention. **UNVERIFIED.**
- I found no current National Assembly or Lagos bill on hospital detention (results were all Kenyan). **UNVERIFIED.**

- Administration of Criminal Justice Act 2015 s.8(2) and the Lagos ACJL bar arrest "merely on a civil wrong or breach of contract". This restrains POLICE, not hospital security. MEDIUM (from memory of the statute; not re-read today).

### B4. How strong is the legal basis?

Strong in principle, thin in authority. A hospital has no power of arrest, debt is not a s.35(1) ground, and false imprisonment is a recognised tort. But: (i) no appellate case applies this to hospitals; (ii) hospitals will say the patient is "awaiting discharge formalities", not detained; (iii) a lawyer should consider whether s.35(1)(b) "obligation imposed upon him by law" could be argued (unlikely: a hospital bill is contractual and there is no "procedure permitted by law"); (iv) enforcement needs a High Court application, which is slow for someone stuck on a ward. The draft internal line "Holding a person over a debt engages the constitutional right to personal liberty" is defensible: "engages" is accurate. Do not upgrade it to "is illegal" in reporter-facing text.

### B5. Corpses and mortuary release

- I found **no Nigerian case or statute** on a hospital refusing to release a body over a medical bill. **UNVERIFIED.** Results were Kenyan (High Court 2025-2026 rulings, Health (Amendment) Bill 2024).
- The PBoR "Patient's Dignity" section (guide p. 7) requires providers to treat "human remains ... with care, consideration and dignity" and to "Ensure comprehensive information, guidance and support to bereaved relatives". **HIGH.** It does not address release against debt.
- A body is not a "person" for s.35, so the liberty argument does not transfer. Mortuary storage fees are a separate, genuinely accruing charge. The bot should state no legal conclusion here and route to legal help.
- Practice evidence: Yates, Brookes and Whitaker, "Hospital Detentions for Non-payment of Fees: A Denial of Rights and Dignity", Chatham House, Dec 2017, https://www.chathamhouse.org/sites/default/files/publications/research/2017-12-06-hospital-detentions-non-payment-yates-brookes-whitaker.pdf (names Nigeria as a country where the practice is "particularly prevalent"; mentions bodies being withheld in several countries). MEDIUM (read via summaries).

## C. Patients' Bill of Rights (PBoR)

- **Issuer:** Consumer Protection Council (CPC), now the Federal Competition and Consumer Protection Commission (FCCPC), with the Federal Ministry of Health and professional bodies. The FCCPC guide says: "The FCCPC, other stakeholders, and the Federal Ministry of Health, have developed the Patients' Bill of Rights". **HIGH**
- **Launch:** 31 July 2018, Abuja, by Vice President Yemi Osinbajo. MEDIUM-HIGH (Nigeria Health Watch, https://nigeriahealthwatch.com/articles/thought-leadership/patients-bill-of-rights-game-changer-or-another-policy-paper/ ; FCCPC hosts the speech at https://fccpc.gov.ng/speech-of-his-excellency-the-vice-president-prof-yemi-osinbajo-san-gcon-at-the-launch-of-pbor-2/ , not opened).
- **Legal status:** not an Act and not a regulation. The guide (p. 1) says: "The PBoR is an aggregation of patients' rights that exist in other instruments including, The Constitution, Consumer Protection Act, Child Rights Act, Freedom of Information Act, National Health Act, the Hippocratic Oath, other professional ethical codes and sundry regulations." **HIGH.** FCCPC treats breaches as consumer-rights matters under the FCCPA 2018. Nigeria Health Watch noted at launch that sanctions "have not been made clear". The bot should call it "the Patients' Bill of Rights issued by the FCCPC and the Federal Ministry of Health", never "the law".
- **Official text:** FCCPC illustrated guide (14-page PDF, text is outlined so it cannot be copied; I read it page by page): https://fccpc.gov.ng/wp-content/uploads/2023/04/PATIENTS-BILL-OF-RIGHTS-ILLUSTRATED-GUIDE.pdf . **HIGH.** The old page http://fccpc.gov.ng/publications/PBOR/ now redirects to the FCCPC home page. Lagos HEFAMAA publishes its own adaptation: https://hefamaa.lagosstate.gov.ng/patient-bill-of-rights/ . **HIGH**
- **Numbering problem:** the official guide does NOT number rights 1 to 12. It has lettered sections A to K, each with numbered Patient Rights, Patient Responsibilities and Provider Responsibilities. The familiar "12 rights" list is the short version. I could not open the official short-version PDF (the law-firm mirror at infusionlawyers.com is dead), so the 1 to 12 order below is from Nigeria Health Watch's report of the launch. Order: MEDIUM. ### C2. The 12-point short list (order per Nigeria Health Watch; MEDIUM)

1. Right to relevant information in a language and manner the patient understands, including diagnosis, treatment, other procedures and possible outcomes.
2. Right to timely access to detailed and accurate medical records and available services.
3. Right to transparent billing and full disclosure of any costs, including recommended treatment plans.
4. Right to privacy, and confidentiality of medical records.
5. Right to clean, safe, and secure healthcare environments.
6. Right to be treated with respect, regardless of gender, race, religion, ethnicity, allegations of crime, disability or economic circumstances.
7. Right to receive urgent, immediate and sufficient intervention and care, in the event of an emergency.
8. Right to reasonable visitation in accordance with prevailing rules and regulations.
9. Right to decline care, subject to prevailing laws and upon full disclosure of the consequences of such a decision.
10. Right to decline or consent to participation in medical research, experimental procedures or clinical trials.
11. Right to quality care in accordance to prevailing standards.
12. Right to complain and express dissatisfaction regarding services received.

### G1. Evidence for each of the four problems

**1. Emergency care refused or delayed for payment or police report**

### H2. Nigeria Data Protection Act 2023 (official copy on the NDPC site: https://ndpc.gov.ng/wp-content/uploads/2024/03/Nigeria_Data_Protection_Act_2023.pdf ). All **HIGH** (read directly).

- **s.65 (interpretation):** "sensitive personal data" includes data relating to an individual's "health status" and "sex life". A report about a patient's treatment is sensitive data, including when a relative or nurse reports about someone else.
- **s.30(1):** sensitive data shall not be processed unless, among other grounds, "(a) data subject has given and not withdrawn consent to the processing for the specific purpose or purposes"; other grounds include vital interests where the subject cannot consent, legal claims, and substantial public interest. Third-party reports (relative, staff) cannot rest on the patient's consent; the pack should minimise, avoid names of patients, and get advice on which s.30 ground applies.
- **s.26:** controller "shall bear the burden of proof for establishing a data subject's consent"; "Silence or inactivity ... shall not constitute consent"; the subject must be told of the right to withdraw before consenting; the request must be "in clear and simple language"; consent must be affirmative, "not based on a pre-selected confirmation", and may be given "in writing, orally, or through electronic means". A chat "YES" works if logged.
- **s.25:** general lawful bases (consent, contract, legal obligation, vital interest, public interest task, legitimate interests with limits).
- **ss.41 to 43:** no transfer out of Nigeria unless the recipient is bound by law, contractual clauses or similar giving "an adequate level of protection" (s.42: "substantially similar" principles), or a s.43 condition applies, e.g. the subject "has provided and not withdrawn consent to such transfer after having been informed of the possible risks". The controller must record the basis for transfer (s.41(2)). Hosting on non-Nigerian cloud, WhatsApp and LLM APIs are all cross-border transfers. Simplest route: explicit informed consent in the opening message plus data-processing terms with vendors.
- Also check: NDPC registration as a data controller "of major importance" (s.44) and the GAID 2025 implementation directive. UNVERIFIED whether a small NGO pilot crosses the threshold.

### H3. Cybercrimes Act s.24 and defamation risk

- **Amended text (Cybercrimes (Prohibition, Prevention, etc.) (Amendment) Act 2024, signed 28 Feb 2024):** s.24(1) now targets a person who knowingly or intentionally sends a message that "is pornographic or he knows to be false, for the purpose of causing a breakdown of law and order, posing a threat to life, or causing such messages to be sent". Penalty reported as a fine up to N7,000,000 or up to 3 years or both. PLAC copy: https://placng.org/i/documents/cybercrimes-prohibition-prevention-etc-amendment-act-2024/ (not opened); wording as quoted by CPJ, Freedom House and Nigerian law firms. MEDIUM-HIGH.
- The ECOWAS Court held on 25 March 2022 (SERAP v Nigeria) that the old s.24 was "arbitrary, vague and repressive" and contrary to art. 9 African Charter. SERAP filed a new suit against the amended section, ECW/CCJ/APP/03/2025. MEDIUM. https://www.premiumtimesng.com/news/top-news/766347-serap-sues-nigerian-govt-at-ecowas-court-over-misuse-of-cybercrimes-act.html
- **Arrests continue despite the amendment.** RSF: at least eight journalists prosecuted under the law after the amendment (https://rsf.org/en/nigeria-least-eight-journalists-prosecuted-under-cybercrime-law-despite-new-amendment). CPJ: four journalists of The Informant247 charged over reporting on corruption at a Kwara State polytechnic, a public INSTITUTION (https://cpj.org/2024/10/nigeria-police-charge-4-journalists-with-cybercrimes-for-corruption-reporting/); three more detained Nov 2025 (https://cpj.org/2025/11/3-nigerian-journalists-detained-on-cybercrime-allegations-despite-reform/amp/). An FIJ reporter was arrested in late 2025 (Premium Times). Citizens: police arraigned two women for "cyberstalking" a bank over a false post (The News, 18 Sept 2026); consumer Chioma Okoli was prosecuted from 2023 over a Facebook review of a tomato paste (from memory, LOW, verify). I found no case of a patient arrested for a post about a PUBLIC hospital. MEDIUM overall.
- **Defamation:** civil libel applies to statements about identifiable people and about corporate bodies with a trading reputation; criminal defamation still exists (Criminal Code s.373 to 375; Lagos Criminal Law 2011 removed it in Lagos: MEDIUM, verify). Government bodies as such are generally thought unable to sue for libel (Derbyshire principle; Nigerian position less settled), but named doctors, nurses and MDs can. Defences: justification (truth), fair comment, qualified privilege.
- Design consequences: publish counts and patterns, never names of staff or patients; keep the "k-anonymity" threshold; label everything "reports received, not verified findings"; offer the hospital a right of reply before publication; let the partner organisation (with its lawyers) be the publisher. The Maitama and Randle episodes show why.

## (2) A1.detention (no legal conclusion)

> A hospital bill is a debt, and there are proper ways to settle a debt. The Patients' Bill of Rights says hospitals have a right to their fees and should use legal means to recover them.
>
> 1. Ask to speak to the hospital's **Social Welfare Unit** (some hospitals call it Medical Social Services). Ask about a payment plan, a waiver, or help from the state's ILERA EKO equity fund.
> 2. Ask for a **written, itemised bill**. You have a right to transparent and itemised billing.
> 3. Ask, calmly, for the **Medical Director's office** to tell you in writing what is needed for discharge.
> 4. For free legal advice, call the Lagos State **Public Defender (OPD/BPD)**, toll-free **0800-006-7352** or **0800-002-7352**.
>
