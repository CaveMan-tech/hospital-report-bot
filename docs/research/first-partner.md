# The first partner in Lagos

Research for slide 12 of the deck. Checked 21 September 2026 by opening the organisations' own
sites and reputable reporting. **Nobody named here has been approached, and nothing here implies
that anyone has agreed to anything.** A fact is stated only if a source was opened; UNCONFIRMED
means it was seen only in a search result or the page would not open. The Center for Reproductive
Rights page on the Oduyoye case was read a second time on the live page and matched.

Not checked for any organisation: governance, independence, or current finances.

## What the partner has to be able to do

1. **Act on a verified pattern**, in its own name: petition, Freedom of Information request,
   litigation, or publication.
2. **Carry the legal risk** of doing so (Cybercrimes Act s.24, defamation; `nigeria-pack.md`, H3).
   That means lawyers or editors on staff.
3. **Reach poor patients**, because they are how the tool gets used at all.
4. **Stay independent of the hospitals** it may have to name.

No single organisation found does all four for all patients. The answer is a lead partner with two
supporting roles.

## The answer

**Lead: a rights organisation with its own lawyers that has already taken a Lagos public hospital to
court.** The closest fit is the Women Advocates Research and Documentation Centre (WARDC), Ikeja.

**For reach: a bill-relief charity already inside the wards.** Health Emergency Initiative (HEI),
Surulere. It cannot be the publisher.

**As amplifier: a newsroom already on this story.** THISDAY's health desk; FIJ. Not the holder of the
data.

**Weaknesses, stated plainly.** It covers women and maternity, not all patients, so a pilot belongs
in a maternity catchment. Its current programmes page is mostly gender-based violence and
governance. Its dedicated maternal-litigation funding dates to 2013 to 2016. Its flagship case
failed on a technicality. No recent hospital-specific publication was found.

### Why they would want it

1. **Their detention case rested on one death**, and was struck out on procedure. Patterns of five or
   more reports per hospital are the systemic evidence, and the route to further complainants, that a
   single-victim case lacks.
2. **Their monitoring is people on foot, paid for by grants.** Twenty monitors across thirty
   hospitals is expensive and stops when the grant does. A chat channel keeps reports arriving
   between grants at almost no cost.
3. **Their demands have outrun their evidence.** In 2015 they asked the Lagos Assembly for a public
   hearing on the strength of three named cases. Hospital-level counts would back the advocacy they
   are still doing in 2026, and give the people they already reach a way to report.

### Why HEI is the reach partner, and not the publisher

Pays bills for indigent patients before and after treatment, including people detained over bills
([what it does](https://hei.org.ng/what-we-do/)); works with the social workers stationed in Lagos
public hospitals and pays hospitals directly; its site reports about 35,700 patients and 91 partner
hospitals. It was named Best Supporting NGO by the Lagos State Health Service Commission in 2019 and
the Governor approved a five-year partnership in 2025
([BusinessDay](https://businessday.ng/life/article/sanwo-olu-hails-hei-as-ngo-targets-n500m-to-tackle-children-malnutrition/)).
It sees these cases daily and could tell patients about the tool. It depends on hospital goodwill, has
no lawyers or editorial process, and is funded by corporates, so it is unlikely to publish anything
about a hospital.

### Why a newsroom is the amplifier, not the holder

- **No route to poor patients.** FIJ's intake is an email address
  ([Report an Injustice](https://fij.ng/report-injustice)); THISDAY shows none. FIJ's Lagos deposit
  story came from a viral TikTok ([FIJ, 3 Apr 2025](https://fij.ng/article/we-didnt-ask-for-n500000-lagos-hospital-denies-wrongdoing-in-pregnant-womans-death/)).
  THISDAY's reporter found her cases by walking the wards.
- **The legal risk has already landed on newsrooms.** Police summoned FIJ's board chair under s.24
  of the Cybercrimes Act in March 2024 ([CPJ](https://cpj.org/2024/04/police-investigate-nigerias-foundation-for-investigative-journalism-after-corruption-coverage/));
  FIJ reporter Daniel Ojukwu was held from 1 to 10 May 2024 ([CPJ](https://cpj.org/2024/05/nigerian-police-secretly-arrest-journalist-daniel-ojukwu-over-critical-report/)).
  An NGO can act on a pattern without publishing it at all.
- **The state rebuts single stories in public** (the Randle statement; `prior-art-and-disputed-cases.md`, 1.2).
- **Appetite is proven.** THISDAY ran three pieces in 2026 on payment barriers across Lagos's 36
  public hospitals ([31 Jan](https://www.thisdaylive.com/2026/01/31/how-single-provider-payment-system-deepens-healthcare-barriers-endangers-lives/),
  [7 Sept](https://www.thisdaylive.com/2026/09/07/viral-outcry-eight-months-after-thisday-expose-lagos-hospitals-payment-woes-persist/),
  [17 Sept](https://www.thisdaylive.com/2026/09/17/when-payment-becomes-a-barrier-to-healthcare-in-lagos-state-owned-hospitals-patients-still-stranded-as-system-failures-persist/)).
  Verified patterns are leads a newsroom re-reports to its own standards.

## What comparable tools teach

- **Transparency International's Advocacy and Legal Advice Centres.** Free, confidential help to
  victims and witnesses of corruption, 400,000 people since 2003; national chapters analyse the cases
  to find hotspots and push reform through litigation and campaigning. One Ghanaian case concerned a
  hospital. Its own account, not an independent evaluation. This is the model Open Ward follows: help
  the person first, then aggregate. [TI](https://www.transparency.org/en/alacs)
- **MomConnect, South Africa.** Complaints about public clinics went to a named facility manager with
  ten days to respond and an escalation path; about 1,300 complaints, over 80% resolved. Feedback
  produced change because it had an owner inside the body that could fix it. Barron and others, BMJ
  Global Health 2018. [PMC5922496](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5922496/)
- **Maji Matone, Tanzania.** Expected 3,000 text messages about broken water points and received 53:
  people were reluctant to report on government, those who collect the water lacked phones, and
  nobody trusted that anything would follow. [SciDev](https://www.scidev.net/sub-saharan-africa/opinions/let-s-make-a-success-of-failures/)

## The funder question

Do not claim that Open Society Foundations funds health rights in Nigeria. It closed its Abuja
office in October 2023 ([Mail & Guardian](https://mg.co.za/news/2025-03-29-the-shrinking-african-footprint-of-the-open-society-foundations/)).
Of the three Africa programmes it launched in July 2025, only "Democratic Futures in Africa" names
Nigeria, and it covers governance and citizen trust in institutions
([OSF](https://www.opensocietyfoundations.org/newsroom/open-society-launches-three-major-africa-initiatives)).
Its Africa page describes funding "citizen audit" programmes that monitor service delivery
([OSF Africa](https://www.opensocietyfoundations.org/what-we-do/regions/africa/)). No patient-rights
grantee appeared in the first page of its Nigeria grants listing. WARDC lists the Open Society
Initiative for West Africa among its funders. The honest fit is citizen monitoring of public services
and trust in institutions.
